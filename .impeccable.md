## Design Context

### Users

DBX serves database developers, DBAs, data engineers, and operators working in dense desktop, web, and self-hosted environments. Their primary jobs are connecting to many database types, writing and comparing queries, inspecting schemas and data, and completing potentially risky data operations with clear feedback and recovery paths. Interfaces should optimize repeated expert workflows without assuming every user is already familiar with DBX.

### Brand Personality

Pragmatic, capable, and calm. DBX should feel like a dependable native database tool: technically serious, fast, and transparent without becoming austere or visually noisy. Product copy is concise and operational rather than promotional.

### Aesthetic Direction

Use a refined, compact, utilitarian application aesthetic informed by mature database IDEs such as DBeaver, DataGrip, and TablePlus while preserving DBX's existing visual language. Keep the established Geist-based typography, neutral and theme-token-driven palette, small configurable corner radii, dense toolbars, and full light/dark/theme support. Prefer flat hierarchy, visible state, and direct manipulation over decorative cards, gradients, glass effects, oversized controls, or marketing-style layouts.

### Design Principles

1. Make the primary workflow immediately visible: expose frequent actions, state, progress, and comparison targets without extra dialogs or hidden menus.
2. Preserve information density with hierarchy: use compact controls, clear active states, truncation, and horizontal scrolling instead of collapsing important choices too early.
3. Keep operational state explicit and reversible: show what is active, allow direct switching and removal, and retain existing safety, backup, and recovery behavior.
4. Reuse established components, spacing, tokens, and interaction patterns across every theme; avoid one-off styling that drifts from the application shell.
5. Support keyboard and pointer workflows equally, including visible focus states, descriptive labels, and reduced-motion-safe transitions.
